Getting started

My Story Begins: Why I Chose to Become an English Teacher

My decision to pursue a degree in English teaching wasn't necessarily something I had planned for a long time. In fact, my original intention was to focus entirely on Software Development, a degree I am still pursuing. However, the rapid development of generative AI and its growing integration into software development workflows have changed the industry quite significantly. Seeing how much the profession is evolving, and questioning whether I wanted to be part of that particular transition, made me reconsider my plans. In a period of uncertainty, I decided to explore a different path.

English teaching seemed like a reasonable starting point. I've been studying English since I was a child, for well over a decade, and it has become a natural part of my everyday life. I use it regularly, both academically and personally, and I've developed a fairly high level of proficiency over the years. I also had the opportunity to learn from many different English teachers throughout my life, so the profession was never entirely unfamiliar to me. Having close family members who work in education, including my mother and my aunt, may have also influenced my decision in ways I hadn't necessarily considered.

Initially, I must admit that I didn't have a particularly deep understanding of what being a teacher actually involved. I think I somewhat arrogantly assumed that teaching was largely a matter of common sense: knowing your subject, explaining it clearly and making sure students understood it. However, studying pedagogy and becoming familiar with the work of authors such as Freire, Piaget and Dewey has challenged that assumption. Learning about cognitive development, different approaches to education and the complexities of the learning process has given me a much broader perspective.

As my coursemate, and now also friend, Eugenia Manasliski, once put it, something might seem like common sense at first, but when you actually take the time to examine it, you realise just how much you had initially overlooked. I think that's a particularly good way of describing my experience so far. Take something as seemingly simple as arranging desks in a circle. You might intuitively feel that this creates a more welcoming environment and encourages students to participate, while also making the traditional hierarchical relationship between teacher and students less prominent. But understanding why that happens, the pedagogical reasoning behind it and the implications it has for classroom dynamics is something entirely different from simply considering it common sense. By studying and reflecting on these ideas, you turn what might have been an intuition into actual knowledge that can inform your teaching practice.

In addition to that, our visits to educational institutions have helped me understand some of the realities of the profession that I hadn't previously considered. If there's one idea that has become particularly important to me, it's that students are people first and students second. Teaching isn't just about what happens inside the classroom or how much content students manage to learn. It's also about recognising who they are, understanding their circumstances and remembering that education involves people, each with their own lives and experiences.

Something that has gradually become clearer to me is that my interest lies much more in teaching itself than in English as a subject. Although I enjoy knowing the language and have spent many years developing my proficiency, I wouldn't necessarily describe English as one of my greatest passions. What interests me is the possibility of helping others learn, developing meaningful relationships with students and having a positive impact beyond the classroom.

I also want to bring something different to education. I'm particularly interested in exploring how technology, artificial intelligence, videogames, music and other resources can be incorporated into the learning process. I don't want to simply reproduce traditional teaching practices because they are familiar or because that's how things have always been done. I want to question them, experiment with new ideas and be willing to challenge established approaches when I believe there are better alternatives.

I'm still at the beginning of this journey, and I know I have a lot to learn. Nevertheless, what initially started as an alternative academic path has gradually become something I'm genuinely interested in pursuing. I'm looking forward to discovering what kind of teacher I can become.

02

Looking ahead

My Expectations About the Teaching Profession

I expect teaching to be a much more demanding profession than it might seem at first. It involves much more than knowing a subject and being able to explain it clearly. There is also planning, assessing students, adapting activities, managing a classroom and dealing with situations that don't always go as expected. I also imagine that a considerable part of the work happens outside the classroom, with assignments to correct, materials to prepare, meetings to attend and administrative responsibilities. I think the workload might be quite different from what people who aren't familiar with the profession imagine.

One of the things I'm most interested in is the opportunity to get to know students and build relationships with them. I think understanding their backgrounds, interests and difficulties is important if we want to create an environment in which everyone feels comfortable participating and making mistakes. However, I expect this to be quite challenging. Students have different abilities, motivations and circumstances, and some may face difficulties that go beyond their education. I imagine that dealing with these situations will require a great deal of patience and empathy, while also understanding that teachers cannot solve every problem their students might have. I also expect to interact with families and other members of the educational community, which adds another dimension to the profession.

Another challenge I expect is dealing with the limitations of the educational system. Large groups, limited resources, strict programmes and institutional requirements might sometimes make it difficult to put certain ideas into practice. There may be things I'd like to do but simply won't have the time or resources for. I also wonder how much freedom teachers actually have to make decisions about their classes and how much of their work is determined by factors outside their control. Working conditions, job stability and salary are also aspects I expect to have a considerable impact on the experience of being a teacher, even though they aren't always discussed as much as teaching methods or classroom activities.

I also expect teaching to involve a lot of continuous learning. I don't think becoming a teacher means you eventually have everything figured out. There will always be new things to learn, different approaches to explore and aspects of your own practice to reconsider. I'm particularly interested in the possibilities that technology and AI can bring to education, and I'd like to experiment with different tools and resources. However, I imagine that not everyone will be equally open to new ideas, and that institutional limitations might sometimes get in the way. I also expect collaboration with other teachers to be important. Sharing ideas, discussing problems and learning from colleagues seems like a valuable way to improve, although I realise that not every institution necessarily encourages this kind of collaboration.

Something else I wonder about is how the profession might change over time. I imagine that some aspects of teaching will eventually become routine, especially after years of preparing lessons, correcting assignments and dealing with similar situations. I hope this doesn't lead to becoming mechanical or losing interest in what I do. At the same time, I expect there will be emotionally demanding moments, conflicts and decisions that won't always have an obvious answer. Teachers have a certain amount of responsibility over their students, not only in terms of what they learn but also in how they feel in the classroom and how they are treated. I imagine that balancing this responsibility with the other demands of the job won't always be easy.

Ultimately, I expect teaching to be a challenging profession, with its fair share of frustrations and disappointments. I don't imagine every lesson will go well or that every idea will work, and I'm sure there will be moments when the reality of the job doesn't match my expectations. Still, I hope the positive aspects will outweigh those difficulties. I look forward to the possibility of learning from others, trying different things and having a positive impact on students' lives. I think what interests me most is that teaching is not something that can be reduced to following a set of instructions; it requires making decisions, adapting to people and situations, and constantly reconsidering what we do.

A message from today

Letter to My Future Self

Dear Fede,

It's strange to think about where we might be ten years from now. By then, we should have quite a bit of experience as English teachers. I wonder if we're happy with what we're doing, whether things turned out as expected and how much we've changed since starting this degree.

One thing I really don't want is for teaching to become just another routine. I know the profession can be demanding, and not every class will be exciting, but I hope we still enjoy what we do. I'd like to think we're still curious, coming up with new ideas and finding things we want to try in the classroom.

I'm also curious about how our teaching philosophy has developed. I'm sure experience has changed the way we see education, and maybe some of the things we strongly believe in today no longer make much sense to us. That's part of growing, I guess. What matters is that we haven't become too attached to our own ideas or stopped questioning the way we do things.

Speaking of ideas, I wonder how many of the more unconventional ones we've actually put into practice. Have we tried anything completely different? Have we developed a project we're particularly proud of? I really don't want us to become teachers who stick to the same old methods just because they're familiar. I'd rather we try something ambitious and fail than spend years wondering what could have happened if we'd given it a chance.

Something that matters even more to me is the kind of relationship we've built with our students. I often think about mom and how much her students care about her. I'd love to have that kind of connection with our own students. I hope we've managed to make at least a small difference in some of their lives.

I also wonder if we've become less hard on ourselves. Knowing us, we might still spend too much time worrying about things that didn't go as planned. But teaching involves making mistakes, having bad lessons and trying things that simply don't work. We don't need to get everything right. We just need to keep learning and be willing to do things differently next time.

And what about the people we started this journey with? We've shared assignments, exams, stressful deadlines and plenty of uncertainty. I'd really like to think we've stayed in touch, kept supporting each other and maybe even had the chance to work together. It would be a shame if those friendships ended when we graduated.

I have no idea what life will look like by then. Maybe our priorities will be completely different, or perhaps we'll have achieved things we haven't even considered yet. Whatever the case, I hope we're happy with where we've ended up and that we can look back at these years knowing we made the most of them.

And one last thing: don't become a stranger to yourself. Don't lose your curiosity, your sense of humour or the excitement you feel when discovering something new. Make time for the things you enjoy, take care of the people who matter to you and don't let work become your whole life. I know we still have a lot to figure out, but I hope you're doing alright.

Take care of yourself, Fede. See you in ten years!

Looking inward

What Makes a Good Teacher

When I think about what makes someone a good teacher, the first things that come to mind are usually knowing the subject, explaining things clearly and keeping students engaged. However, the more I learn about education, the more I realise there is much more to it than that. Patricia Miller's Ten Characteristics of a Good Teacher explores several qualities that go beyond subject knowledge and teaching skills. Some were things I had already considered, while others made me think about aspects of teaching I hadn't paid much attention to before.

One thing I particularly agree with is the importance of building positive relationships with students. Having studied English for many years, I know how frustrating it can be to understand something but struggle to express it. A teacher's patience and encouragement can make a huge difference. Students need to feel comfortable participating and making mistakes without being judged. At the same time, I agree with Miller that teachers should challenge their students. The important thing is finding a balance between pushing them to improve and providing the support they need.

I also liked her point about getting to know students as individuals. Their interests, backgrounds and experiences can be valuable when planning lessons and choosing activities. This connects with treating everyone fairly. I think it's easy to give more attention to students who participate frequently or seem more motivated, but the others shouldn't be overlooked. Not everyone needs the same kind of support, and recognising that is an important part of teaching.

Creativity and humour are two other qualities I'd like to bring into my own classroom. I wouldn't want lessons to become repetitive or follow the same formula every day. Games, music, discussions and technology offer many possibilities beyond textbooks and traditional exercises. However, humour needs to be used carefully. It can make students feel more relaxed, but inappropriate jokes or sarcasm can have the opposite effect.

Some of Miller's ideas also made me think about the limits of a teacher's responsibilities. She mentions being available to students outside class and leaving personal problems at the door. While I agree that teachers should be approachable, I don't think they have to be available all the time or pretend to be emotionless. They have their own lives and responsibilities too.

Overall, I agree with most of Miller's ideas, although putting these qualities into practice is easier said than done. As someone still studying to become a teacher, I know I have a lot to learn. For me, being a good teacher involves much more than knowing your subject. It also means understanding the people in front of you and recognising the impact you can have on their lives.
