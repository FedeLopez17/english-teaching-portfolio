01

Professional learning

Selected Readings and Their Relevance

Throughout my first year of teacher education, I have come across different authors and readings that have made me think about what being a teacher actually involves.

Some were part of our general pedagogical courses, while others were recommended by our English professors through their course bibliographies. I wanted to include both because understanding how people learn and what education should look like is just as important as learning how to teach English.

One of the authors we studied extensively was Jean Piaget. I found his ideas about cognitive development particularly interesting, especially concepts such as assimilation and accommodation. Before studying his theories, I had not given much thought to how differently children and adolescents think as they grow. His work helped me understand that learning involves connecting new information with what we already know and sometimes changing the way we understand things. It also made me realise why teachers need to consider students' previous knowledge instead of assuming that everyone will understand an explanation in the same way.

Lev Vygotsky's ideas added another perspective. While Piaget helped me understand how individuals construct knowledge, Vygotsky emphasises the role of social interaction. His concept of the Zone of Proximal Development shows how students can achieve more with guidance from a teacher or support from classmates. This connects with my own experience, since discussing topics and explaining things to one another have often helped me understand them better. It also reinforces my interest in encouraging collaboration, particularly in language teaching, where communication is central to learning.

These ideas connect with Paulo Freire's Pedagogy of the Oppressed, although his focus is more on the purpose of education and the relationship between teachers and students. I was particularly interested in his criticism of education based on simply transferring information. I have always felt there is more to education than memorising content and reproducing it in exams. His emphasis on dialogue and critical thinking reflects the kind of classroom I would like to create: one where students can question things, express their opinions, and contribute rather than just listen and take notes.

Moving towards English language teaching, Jeremy Harmer's How to Teach English helped me connect these broader ideas with everyday classroom practice. I focused on three chapters about good teachers, good learners, and classroom management. The first two made me think about the importance of building relationships with students and creating an environment where they feel comfortable participating and making mistakes. This connects with Vygotsky's emphasis on support and interaction, as well as Freire's view of students as active participants. The chapter on classroom management introduced me to practical details I had not considered much before, such as the teacher's voice and movement, seating arrangements, and transitions between activities.

H. Douglas Brown's Teaching by Principles explores the ideas behind different approaches to language teaching. I am particularly interested in motivation, language acquisition, and individual differences, as these topics help us think about how students learn rather than simply what we teach. I often think about how to make lessons interesting without losing sight of their purpose. I like using games and creative activities, for example, but they should contribute to learning rather than just entertain. Harmer's The Practice of English Language Teaching complements Brown's approach by focusing on practical aspects such as lesson planning, teaching language skills, and assessment. Together, these books help connect the reasoning behind teaching decisions with their application in the classroom.

I also wanted to include two shorter readings connected to my personal interests. The first is Nik Peachey's article Generative AI and the Language Classroom. Having an interest in technology, I find the possibilities of AI in education particularly interesting. I would like to explore how these tools can help teachers create materials, adapt activities, and give students more opportunities to practise English. At the same time, their use raises questions about student autonomy and how to make sure technology supports learning instead of doing the work for students. This connects with Brown's ideas about choosing methods and resources according to their actual contribution to learning.

The second reading is the British Council article Using Music and Songs. Music is something I enjoy, and I would genuinely like to bring it into my future classroom. Songs can help students practise listening, pronunciation, and vocabulary, but they can also lead to discussions and other forms of interaction. I see this as another way of connecting students' interests with language learning while keeping activities meaningful.

Ultimately, these readings have helped me connect broader ideas about education with the specific decisions involved in teaching English. Even though I am only at the beginning of my teacher education, I am starting to see how these different perspectives can come together in the kind of teacher I would like to become.

02

Teacher perspectives

A Day in the Life of an English Teacher

The following accounts are based on interviews with four English teachers: Bruno, Noel, Melina and Alejandra. They all work in different contexts and have different levels of experience, so their routines are not exactly the same.

However, their testimonies give us a better idea of what a teacher's everyday life can look like and everything that goes on outside the classroom.

FIRST TESTIMONY

Bruno – Teaching at UTU Agrario

Bruno usually starts his day around 7:00 AM, when he attends classes at IFD, where he is studying to become an English teacher. On Mondays and Fridays, he also teaches eighth-grade EBI at UTU Agrario.

His classes take place around midday on Mondays and at 3:00 PM on Fridays, adding up to three teaching hours per week. Before each lesson, he prepares his materials and plans activities based on what students are working on.

On Fridays, he also takes part in coordination meetings with teachers from other subjects. They share ideas, discuss upcoming activities and look for ways to include English in projects involving different areas.

Combining his studies with teaching can be demanding, but he enjoys being able to put what he learns at IFD into practice and gain experience in a real school environment.

SECOND TESTIMONY

Noel – Teaching Practice and Working at Anglo

Noel has quite a busy routine. Besides working at Anglo and doing her teaching practice at a secondary school, she is also studying at IFD Mercedes. She also has to balance all of this with being a mother and taking part in other extracurricular activities.

At secondary school, she gets to put into practice what she learns at IFD. Working with different groups has shown her that students have different personalities and ways of learning, so she cannot approach every class in the same way.

At Anglo, she works with smaller groups, which allows her to get to know her students better and understand their individual needs. Although the teaching approach is somewhat more traditional, she finds the experience useful for developing classroom management skills.

She also thinks collaboration with other teachers is really important. Sharing ideas and experiences makes her feel supported and gives her new resources and strategies to use in class. Even though balancing all her responsibilities can be challenging, she considers these experiences very valuable for her development as a teacher.

THIRD TESTIMONY

Melina – Teaching in Different Institutions and Towns

Melina works at several institutions in different locations, so travelling between classes is a regular part of her day. On Mondays, she gets up at 6:45 AM and starts teaching at 7:30 at a private secondary school in Libertad, where she works until 10:15.

After that, she goes back home, prepares some materials and makes lunch before heading to Puntas de Valdez, where she teaches from 1:45 PM until 7:45 PM. Once she gets home, she still has her responsibilities as a mother and at home.

However, not every day looks the same. Some days, for example, she starts at 7:45 AM with her Anglo classes in Puntas de Valdez. After finishing at 10:45, she goes home for lunch and then teaches primary-school students in Libertad from 12:45 to 2:45 PM. She then has to travel around 10 kilometres back to Puntas de Valdez for another class, before returning to Libertad for her last Anglo class, which ends at 7:00 PM.

As she explained, almost every day is different depending on the classes she has. Her routine shows how tiring it can be to work in different places, especially when you also have to take travelling time and family responsibilities into account.

FOURTH TESTIMONY

Alejandra – Teaching and Pedagogical Guidance

Alejandra is an experienced teacher (grade 6), currently working at the Liceo Departamental and in Áreas Pedagógicas. Her day starts at 8:00 AM and sometimes lasts until 7:00 PM, with a one-hour break around midday. She currently has a twenty-hour position as a Profesora Orientadora Pedagógica (POP), while the rest of her working hours are dedicated to teaching classes.

One thing she highlighted was the importance of planning ahead. She has made a habit of setting aside one day a week, usually Saturdays, to prepare her lessons for the entire following week. She considers this one of the most important habits a teacher can develop because it makes the rest of the week much easier. Once she gets to class, she already knows what she is going to do instead of having to figure everything out at the last minute.

Her experience shows how important organisation can be when dealing with a demanding workload. Having a clear plan for the week allows her to feel more relaxed and focus on her classes.

What the Four Testimonies Have in Common

Although the four teachers have quite different routines, there are some things they all have to deal with. Teaching involves much more than the hours spent in the classroom. There are materials to prepare, lessons to plan and other responsibilities that need attention outside teaching hours.

Their testimonies also show how demanding the profession can be, especially when working at different institutions or having other commitments outside school. Their schedules can be quite busy, and sometimes they have to adapt when things do not go as planned.

Another thing that stood out to me is how important it is to learn from everyday experience. Whether it is through coordination meetings, working with different groups or simply getting used to the demands of the job, teachers keep developing their skills. Alejandra's approach to weekly planning is also a good example of how experience can help you find ways to make your workload more manageable.

Their input definitely gave me a better idea of what being an English teacher actually involves. There is no single routine that applies to everyone, since it depends on the institutions, the students and each teacher's personal circumstances. Still, being organised, flexible and willing to keep learning seem to be important in all cases.

03

Personal perspective

What I Imagine My Future Life as a Teacher Will Look Like

After hearing about their experiences, I've started thinking about what my own routine might look like as an English teacher.

I imagine working at one or two secondary schools, with classes spread across the morning and afternoon.

I'd probably get up around 6:30 AM, have breakfast and head to my first school. I'd spend the morning teaching different groups, taking attendance, talking to colleagues and dealing with the usual things that come up during a school day.

After lunch, I'd either stay at the same institution or travel to another school for my afternoon classes. I'm sure not every lesson would go as planned, and I'd sometimes have to change activities or find different ways to deal with a particular group.

By the time I got home, I'd probably be tired, but my workday wouldn't necessarily be over. There would still be assignments to correct, lessons to prepare and materials to organise. I'd also want to make time for other things, like exercising, practising guitar, playing videogames or spending time with my family. I imagine finding that balance won't always be easy, but it's something I'd like to work towards.

04

Professional development

My Experience at the Lit Fest

An academic event that gave me the chance to explore other people's work, exchange ideas and imagine participating myself in the future.

I really enjoyed attending the Lit Fest. It was an academic event where students, teachers and other professionals had the opportunity to give presentations, share their work and exchange ideas about topics related to English, literature and education. I honestly didn't know exactly what to expect, but I was pleasantly surprised by how well the students and all the speakers did. It was interesting to see the different topics they had prepared and how they presented their ideas.

One of the presentations I attended was Whose Voice Is It? How AI-Generated Language and Human Speech Shape Each Other, by Facundo Hernández. I found it particularly interesting, especially the idea that AI-generated language can influence the way humans communicate, just as human language influences AI. I had never really thought about that relationship in those terms. Since AI is becoming increasingly present in education, I think it is important for us, as future teachers, to understand these changes and consider how they might affect the way people learn and use language.

I also enjoyed the general atmosphere of the event. I liked exploring the venue next to CERP in Colonia and meeting students from other groups and institutions. I even had the opportunity to chat with some of them, which was nice. At first, I felt a little out of place because I'm sometimes socially awkward, especially around people I don't know. However, that feeling didn't last long, and I soon felt comfortable. The whole experience made me feel more connected to the profession I am preparing for.

While I was there, I started imagining what it would be like to present something of my own. To be honest, I find the idea quite scary, but that's exactly why I think it would be a great opportunity to step out of my comfort zone and challenge myself. Presenting at an event like this involves much more than knowing English. You need to organize your thoughts, communicate your ideas clearly, engage with an audience and, above all, deal with your nerves.

Although I have been studying English for many years and feel comfortable with the language, expressing myself orally in front of other people is still something I struggle with. It is not really a matter of vocabulary or grammar. My biggest obstacles are nervousness, anxiety and shyness. Sometimes I know exactly what I want to say, but when I have to say it in front of an audience, I find it difficult to get my ideas across. Still, I'm confident that I have what it takes to prepare and deliver an outstanding presentation. I just need to learn how to manage my anxiety so that it doesn't get in the way.

I do feel that I have made some progress already. Some of my classmates have even told me that they notice a difference and that I perform better each time. I don't always realize it myself, so hearing that is encouraging. I know I still have a lot to work on, but at least I feel that I'm moving in the right direction.

That is why participating in a future edition of the Lit Fest would mean a lot to me. Being able to prepare a presentation and share my ideas in English in front of an audience would be more than just an academic achievement. It would be tangible proof of my personal progress. I would be able to look back at this first experience, when I was simply sitting in the audience and imagining myself participating someday, and see how far I had come.

Connecting ideas

Pedagogy and Development Psychology: Putting Theory into Practice

An opportunity to put some of the ideas from our courses into practice through an activity prepared for students from Áreas Pedagógicas.

As part of our Pedagogy and Development Psychology courses, we took part in an event where we had the opportunity to put into practice some of the things we had been learning. Students from Áreas Pedagógicas, an educational programme that offers young people who have faced difficulties continuing their studies another opportunity to complete basic secondary education, visited our facility. They were divided into groups and went from one stand to another, taking part in the different activities we had prepared.

Along with Ximena and Noel, I prepared an English vocabulary game. First, students had to match cards in a memory game. Then, they listened to short audio recordings and tried to identify the actions they represented, using mime, drawing or playdough to show their answers. We decorated our stand with balloons and signs, and put quite a bit of effort into preparing the materials. I made the wooden memory cards with my uncle, who does woodworking, and I also created a website with the different audio recordings for the second challenge. We wanted everything to look good and make the activity as engaging as possible.

Before the event, I was excited but honestly didn't know what to expect. I wasn't sure how the students would react or how well we would handle the activity ourselves. I also felt a bit nervous about leading a group. However, once we got started, I gradually relaxed and started enjoying it more. Interacting with the students was one of the best parts of the experience. They seemed to have a good time, and one moment that stuck with me was when a student asked if he could take home the drawing he had made. It was nice to see that something we had prepared had made such an impression on him.

I also found myself thinking about some of the things we had discussed in class. Piaget's ideas about active learning could be seen in how students had to observe, remember and make connections instead of just memorising words. Vygotsky's emphasis on social interaction was also relevant, since students shared ideas and helped each other. I thought about Erikson, too, especially when some students seemed hesitant to participate or said they didn't know English. It made me realise how important it is to make students feel comfortable enough to give things a try, even when they aren't sure of the answer.

One thing I learned is that activities rarely go exactly as planned. We had prepared a script to explain the rules, but once we were with the students, we just explained everything in our own words and adjusted depending on the group. Some already knew how to play memory, while others needed a bit more help. We also realised that we had to consider different learning needs. For example, some students could not read, so we had to make sure they could still understand the instructions and participate in the challenges. We also had to change the original setup because fewer students showed up than we had expected. There was even a moment when a student interpreted an audio recording meant to represent hammering as someone knocking on a door. We accepted the answer because it was a reasonable interpretation. These moments reminded me of Meirieu's ideas about the teacher's role: preparation matters, but you also need to be able to adapt to what is happening in the classroom and to the students in front of you.

I also really enjoyed working with Noel and Ximena. I think we made a great team and complemented each other well. We could share ideas openly, and even when we didn't initially agree on something, we managed to talk it through and find a solution. It made the whole process more enjoyable, and I think working together helped us come up with a better activity.

This experience gave me a better idea of what teaching actually involves. Before doing this activity, I could understand these things in theory, but experiencing them was different. Explaining instructions, keeping students engaged, noticing how they react and knowing when to change something all require practice. I started out feeling unsure about how I would manage, but by the end I felt much more comfortable and genuinely enjoyed being there with the students. It made me realise that this is something I want to keep experiencing and getting better at as I continue my teacher training.
