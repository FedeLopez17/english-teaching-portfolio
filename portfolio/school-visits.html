01

First-hand experience

Visits to Educational Institutions

As part of the course, we visited two educational institutions in Mercedes. These visits gave us the opportunity to learn more about their organisation, facilities, educational proposals and the realities of their communities.

Both experiences helped me look at schools from a different perspective. Rather than simply seeing them as places where lessons take place, I began paying more attention to the people, relationships, projects and circumstances that shape everyday school life.

The following sections bring together my observations and personal reflections from each visit.

02

Visit 01 · UTU

01

Washington Lockhart Technical School

Tuesday, 13 October · UTU

On Tuesday, 13 October, I visited Washington Lockhart Technical School (UTU) with my classmates and two of our professors. We were welcomed by Elena, one of the school's administrative employees, who showed us around, explained how the institution works and answered our questions.

This was a particularly meaningful experience for me, as I was already very familiar with the institution. I had been a student there for three years, studying music, so I knew the place, some of its spaces and its general atmosphere. I had a very good experience during my time there, and I can only speak positively about the institution. It was also interesting to return as a teacher-training student and look at the school from a different perspective, paying attention to aspects of its organisation, infrastructure and educational approach that I might not have noticed as a student.

The school is directed by María Selva Martinol and has around 489 students distributed across 23 groups, with 13 in the morning and 10 in the afternoon. It offers several educational programmes, including Basic Education (7th, 8th and 9th grades), FPB (Basic professional training) and EMT (Technological Intermediate Education), with different areas such as gastronomy, music, sports, health and robotics. I found this variety particularly interesting because it gives students different opportunities depending on their interests and future plans.

We also learned about the school's organisation and the relationships between its staff members. There seems to be a very good relationship between the management team, the administrative staff and the adscriptos, who also keep in touch with students' families. The institution has a Comisión de Fomento, which manages government funds and organises events to raise additional money. Throughout the year, the school also holds music performances, camps, educational visits and exhibitions where students can show their work.

The building has been renovated and includes a spacious courtyard with a basketball court, a dining hall, a computer room, a library, a multimedia room and a music classroom. Most classrooms have televisions. I particularly liked seeing the music classroom again, as it brought back memories of my time there. The building also has ramps, adapted bathrooms and tactile paving for people with visual impairments. However, there is no elevator, which means students with reduced mobility cannot access the second floor. This is one aspect of the infrastructure that could be improved.

One of the projects we heard about focused on improving relationships between students. They worked together to decorate the school for the arrival of spring, which encouraged some students who were usually more reserved or did not interact much to participate and get to know one another. I liked this example because it shows how activities outside regular lessons can contribute to creating a better school environment. I think this is something worth considering as a future teacher: learning is not only about academic content, but also about relationships and coexistence.

We also visited the gastronomy facilities. The course is attended by students aged 14 to 21, and the morning group has around ten regular students. The kitchen is spacious and well-equipped for practical lessons. Students wear uniforms and follow specific hygiene rules, including washing their hands before entering. It was interesting to see how the space is organised to suit the activities they carry out, rather than following the traditional classroom arrangement.

Other activities include a school garden connected to Biology lessons, as well as camps and educational outings. I think these experiences are valuable because they allow students to learn in different ways and outside the classroom.

Overall, I really enjoyed the visit. It was nice to return to a place where I had spent three years and so many good moments, but also to discover things I hadn't paid much attention to before. I left with an even better impression of the institution. More importantly, the visit reminded me that a school is much more than its classrooms and lessons. There are people, relationships, projects and many other things that contribute to students' experiences. I think understanding this is an important part of becoming a teacher.

Visit 02 · Secondary

02

Liceo N.º 4 – Centro María Espínola

Tuesday, 20 October · Secondary Education

On Tuesday, 20 October, we visited Liceo N.º 4, a María Espínola Centre, together with our classmates and two professors. We were welcomed by Fabiana, a teacher who also coordinates some of the school's workshops, and she guided us around the institution while explaining how it works.

The school offers Basic Secondary Education and has approximately 260 students and 50 teachers. It follows a full-time model, with students attending from 7:30 a.m. to 3:30 p.m. During the day, they receive breakfast, lunch and an afternoon snack, and participate in different workshops and activities. The institution serves a community facing social and economic difficulties. We were told that many students receive little support from their families with their education, and some even arrive from primary school with difficulties in reading and writing. Others have repeated the same year several times.

One thing that caught my attention was the importance given to creating routines and habits. Students have designated times for meals and eat in groups according to their year. Lunch, for example, lasts around 20 minutes. We were told that getting students used to these routines is a slow process that requires consistency and patience. The meals are provided by an external company and arrive frozen, which is another aspect of the organisation of the centre.

We also visited several of the school's facilities. There is a spacious library, a psychologist's office, a sexual health advisor's office, laboratories and different workshop spaces, including facilities for gastronomy, sewing and hairdressing. One of the laboratories particularly caught my attention. It contained students' handmade projects, Minecraft-themed crafts, World Cup flags decorating the ceiling and even 3D printers. I liked seeing these examples of students' work because they showed the variety of interests and skills being explored at the institution. There are also support classes for students who need additional help.

Another aspect I found particularly interesting was the school's approach to educational outings. We were told that many students feel somewhat excluded from the rest of Mercedes. They tend to spend most of their time within their own neighbourhood and rarely visit other parts of the city. Therefore, these outings are not necessarily about travelling far away, but rather about helping students discover places they might not normally visit, such as the theatre or the promenade. I really liked this idea because it shows that even a visit to somewhere relatively close can be a meaningful experience when it allows students to explore their own city and feel more connected to it.

Of course, the institution also faces several difficulties. Violence and conflicts between students are ongoing concerns, and the school has introduced stricter rules regarding mobile phone use, eventually prohibiting them in August due to incidents involving aggression and online harassment. Family involvement is another challenge, with relatively low participation in some school meetings. There are also limitations regarding the building and its resources: the teachers' room does not currently have a computer, some classrooms lack air conditioning, only two of the four bathrooms are in use, and the school does not have an assembly hall.

Overall, this visit gave me a different perspective on the realities teachers can face. What stood out to me was the amount of support the institution tries to provide despite its limitations. The full-time model, workshops, meals, psychological support and educational outings all play an important role in students' lives. I particularly appreciated the effort to give students opportunities to experience things outside their usual environment. It made me realise that teaching involves much more than what happens in the classroom. Understanding students' circumstances, helping them develop routines and giving them opportunities they might otherwise miss are also important parts of education.
